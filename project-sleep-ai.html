Sleep Cycle · 2024–25 AI in Design Practice

Using AI across every layer of the work

From brand imagery to motion to copy — how AI expanded what a small design team could make.

Role
Product Designer
Team
Design, marketing
Timeline
2024–25
Scope
Brand, product, campaigns
My focus
AI tooling, motion direction, copy systems
In short

AI became a working part of the toolkit at every level — not as a shortcut, but as a way to reach visual and written territory that a lean team couldn't have accessed otherwise.

Midjourney produced brand stills and motion assets for paywalls. AI video explored onboarding. A Claude model trained on Sleep Cycle's tone shaped copy at scale. And AI prototyping made it possible to simulate experiences that Figma simply couldn't express.

01 — Midjourney as art direction

Brand visuals made in a prompt

Sleep Cycle needed a visual language for its paywall moments — something warm, dreamlike, aspirational. Commissioning photography at that quality and volume wasn't feasible. Midjourney was.

The images weren't used as-is. They went through art direction rounds — prompts refined across many iterations to hit a specific tone: not generic stock, not sterile digital, but something that felt like a waking dream. The bed and window image became a paywall background. The starry sky anchored a soft paywall rollout.

▣ Motion · autoplay · loops
02 — AI motion

Slow, ambient, alive

The Midjourney sequences were the most distinctive asset in the paywall — looping AI-generated video clips with a quality no motion designer could have produced at this pace or cost.

The brief was restraint: nothing that distracts, everything that creates mood. Each sequence was chosen for its almost imperceptible movement — a sense that something is gently breathing. The effect on paywall screens was a premium feel that static imagery couldn't match.

03 — Back to school campaign

First video campaign. It worked.

The back-to-school campaign was Sleep Cycle's first attempt at video advertising. The assets were AI-generated — Midjourney imagery animated into a campaign format.

We didn't know if video would convert better than static. It did. The campaign performed well and shifted how the team thought about video as a channel going forward.

04 — AI copy model

Training Claude on Sleep Cycle's voice

Sleep Cycle had a distinct tone — calm, considered, never clinical. Maintaining it across a growing surface area of paywall states, notifications, and onboarding copy was a constant editorial challenge.

I trained a Claude model on Sleep Cycle's existing copy guidelines and writing guides — a corpus of approved copy and editorial principles. The result was a model that could generate on-brand first drafts at scale: fast enough to explore many directions, close enough in tone to be a useful starting point rather than something to fight against.

This wasn't about replacing copy decisions. It was about making the first draft less of a blank page — and freeing up editorial attention for the harder calls about what to actually say, not how to say it.

The constraint

Copy at scale always drifts. Every new screen written in isolation pulls slightly away from the centre of the brand's voice. A trained model doesn't drift — it holds the established tone as a baseline, which is often more useful than a blank model with a long brief.

05 — AI for complex prototyping

Simulating what Figma can't express

Some experiences are too dynamic or conversational to prototype meaningfully in Figma. A screen-by-screen flow can't capture how an AI-moderated interaction actually feels — the variability, the pacing, the sense of being in dialogue.

I used AI to prototype these flows: generating representative conversation branches, simulating how a system might respond to different user inputs, and creating enough fidelity to test with users without building the real thing. This was particularly relevant for exploring conversational onboarding concepts — where the question-and-answer structure is the experience, not a set of screens.

The output wasn't a polished prototype. It was a testable sketch — enough to get genuine reactions from users, which is what the research needed.

Where it applied

Conversational onboarding flows where branching logic exceeded what Figma's prototyping could handle. Exploring how a product might respond to different sleep concerns with personalised follow-up — not just route to a different screen, but generate a different kind of reply.

What it unlocked

Faster signal on whether a conversational model was worth building. Rather than commissioning engineering time to build a prototype, we could get user reactions to a simulation, decide if the concept had legs, and then spec properly. The cost of being wrong dropped significantly.

Reflection

AI as a multiplier, not a replacement

The consistent pattern across every AI application was the same: AI expanded the range of what was possible with the resources available, and creative direction determined whether the result was any good.

Midjourney images only worked because someone decided which prompts to pursue, which iterations had the right tone, and how to integrate them into a visual system. The copy model only saved time because there was an editorial standard for it to be trained against. The prototypes only generated useful research because they were used in well-designed sessions with real users.

The tools lowered the cost of exploring. They didn't lower the bar for what the exploration needed to be.
