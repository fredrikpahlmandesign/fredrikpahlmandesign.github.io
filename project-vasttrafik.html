Västtrafik runs public transport across the Gothenburg region — trains, trams, and buses — and was rolling out a new generation of onboard ticket validators. The existing touch interface had been heavily criticised by drivers and passengers alike: it was, in effect, the layout of a physical button panel reproduced on a touchscreen, with almost nothing adapted for how people actually read and tap a screen.

This was my first project as a newly hired UX designer at inUse. Working alongside a visual designer, I was asked to rebuild the interaction model from the ground up — turning a dense, abbreviation-heavy interface into something a commuter could understand and act on in the few seconds it takes to board.

The product

Before — the existing UI: a button panel painted onto a touchscreen.

After — a few iterations along the way, built around one message and one action per screen.

The original interface packed a dozen physical-style buttons onto a small screen, each carrying a one- or two-letter label — "T", "V", "S", "K", "Å" — left over from a button-based hardware design that had simply been repainted for a touchscreen. Reading it meant first learning what the abbreviations stood for.

We rebuilt the interaction model around a single legible message and a single next action per screen: which stop you're at, which zone or ticket type you're choosing, and a large tap-the-card affordance that stayed consistent whether you were validating one ticket, a group, or undoing a mistake.

Colour did the work the labels used to do — a calm blue for "you're on board," a brighter yellow for an action waiting on you. A single "+" gave access to more detail without cluttering the default view.

The challenge

Designing for three seconds on a moving bus

A ticket validator isn't a screen people sit down with. It's used standing, often one-handed, holding a bag or a child, sometimes while the vehicle is already pulling away from the stop. Whatever we designed had to be understood at a glance and operated correctly under time pressure — by everyone, not just confident smartphone users.

That meant testing earlier and more broadly than was typical for this kind of project, including with people who have low vision — a group the existing button-panel design had effectively ignored, despite running on a touchscreen with no tactile feedback at all.

"We didn't just usability test it in a lab. We put a working prototype on real vehicles and watched 600 commuters use it on their actual morning commute."

As the design matured, we ran increasingly realistic rounds of testing — from paper prototypes and lab sessions through to a live field test: a working prototype installed on vehicles in regular service, used by more than 600 passengers over their normal commute. That's about as close to "ready to ship" as user testing gets.

How I worked

01

Diagnosing a button panel pretending to be a touchscreen

The first job was diagnosis, not design: figuring out why the existing interface read as confusing, and separating what was genuinely necessary information from what was simply an inherited convention from button-based hardware. Most of the complexity turned out to be the latter.

02

Testing in increasingly realistic rounds

We moved from paper prototypes to lab usability sessions to a live in-traffic field test, raising the stakes and the realism at each stage. Testing included visually impaired participants throughout — not as a separate accessibility pass at the end, but as part of every round.

03

Partnering closely with a visual designer

I worked side by side with a visual designer to translate every interaction decision onto a small, low-resolution, sunlight-readable display — balancing legibility and colour contrast against the hardware's real constraints, not an idealised version of the screen.

Outcome

What I took from this

01
Test in the real environment, not a proxy for it

The field test with 600 commuters taught me more about what "intuitive" actually means under real conditions — a moving vehicle, time pressure, one free hand — than any lab session could. I've carried that bias toward real-context testing into every project since.

02
Accessibility isn't an edge case

Designing with visually impaired participants from the start showed how much of the original interface's complexity was a barrier for everyone, not just a subset of users. Designing for the hardest case usually improves things for everyone else too.

03
Good design doesn't guarantee delivery

An early, formative lesson: a well-tested, signed-off design can still die for reasons that have nothing to do with the design itself — here, a supplier unable to deliver the build. Shipping depends on more than getting the UX right.
