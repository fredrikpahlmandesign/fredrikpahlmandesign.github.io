Sleep Cycle · 2024–25 Conversion & Experimentation

Moving the metrics

Three experiments on the subscription model — one clean win, two strategic bets worth making.

In short

The subscription model hadn't changed in years. Discovery findings gave us the evidence to run experiments — three of them, at different scales and risk levels, all aimed at the same problem: too many users never getting past the payment screen.

One produced a clean uplift. One ran into headwinds from other company events and couldn't be read clearly. One was a deliberate strategic trade-off — accepted because the long-term bet was worth more than the short-term conversion cost.

01 — Onboarding UI reel

The experiment that landed

The discovery practice had surfaced a clear pattern: users who understood what Sleep Cycle actually did in the first session were significantly more likely to return. The onboarding wasn't showing them enough.

The UI reel was the response — a motion-forward onboarding sequence that showed the app's core loop in action rather than describing it. Designed to demonstrate value before asking for commitment. Built on interview evidence rather than gut feel.

02 — Reverse trial

Letting users earn the ask

The hard paywall asked users to pay before they'd felt any value. The reverse trial flipped it: premium unlocked upfront, payment asked later.

The concept was grounded in a real insight from discovery — many users who reached the paywall said they needed to know the app worked for them before committing. The reverse trial was designed to solve that directly: let them experience premium, then present the subscription as a continuation rather than a gamble.

The experiment ran, but the results couldn't be read cleanly. Other significant changes were happening across the company at the same time, and the signal drowned in the noise. It remains a concept we believed in — one that deserved a cleaner run.

The framing

An inconclusive experiment isn't a failed one. The reverse trial taught us about the measurement environment as much as the concept itself — and the hypothesis behind it informed how we approached the soft paywall that followed.

03 — Soft paywall rollout

The biggest bet

Discovery had surfaced a consistent pattern: users who hit the hard paywall before getting any value from the app were leaving. A lot of them. The soft paywall was the strategic response — let users in, then show them why Premium was worth paying for.

It was always going to lower conversion rate. You're replacing a forced decision with an optional one. The bet was that more users reaching the aha moment would outweigh the ones who'd pay up front under pressure — and that the long-term retention picture would look better.

The trade-off was the strategy

Activation doubled. More users moved past the paywall and into the product. Conversion dropped — as expected, as planned. The question was always whether the users who activated would be better users over time.

This wasn't a mistake caught in hindsight. The metrics moved exactly as the model predicted. That's what a deliberate strategic bet looks like.

Reflection

Experiments as evidence, not answers

The three experiments didn't produce three clean wins. That was never the goal. They produced evidence — about what users needed, what the model could sustain, and where the constraints really were.

The UI reel showed that showing value early changes behaviour. The reverse trial showed that the measurement environment matters as much as the hypothesis. The soft paywall showed that the team was willing to accept a short-term conversion cost in exchange for a better long-term picture — and that's a meaningful shift in how product strategy gets made.

None of these experiments would have been possible without the discovery practice behind them. The metrics moved because the team understood the problem well enough to design the right interventions.
