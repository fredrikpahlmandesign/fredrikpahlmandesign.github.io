In short

I built and ran a continuous discovery practice at Sleep Cycle — weekly user sessions, embedded into the product team's rhythm. When the sessions paused over summer, a major experiment shipped without user input and came back flat. Resuming the practice — and scaling it with AI-moderated interviews — is what finally diagnosed what went wrong.

The practice didn't just produce research. It changed how the team made decisions — and produced the strategic tools, including a customer journey model, that shaped product direction for over a year.

01 — The context

Sharp instincts, thin evidence

Sleep Cycle was full of smart people with good product intuitions. But decisions were made mostly by feel. Research happened reactively — when something went wrong, or when a major feature needed sign-off.

There was no regular contact with real users. No cadence. No way to build institutional knowledge about who our users were and what they needed. Discovery was a project, not a practice.

My goal was to change that — to turn research from an occasional deliverable into the connective tissue of how the team worked.

02 — Building the cadence

Wednesday mornings, every week

We settled on weekly sessions — small groups, rotating guests from across the product team, a structured but open conversation with real users.

Embedded, not separate

The sessions weren't a research phase that ran alongside the team's work. They were part of how the team worked. Designers attended. PMs attended. Over six months, the Wednesday cadence became a standing fixture — a place where the team heard directly from users before committing to solutions.

Building compound knowledge

What makes continuous discovery valuable isn't any single session — it's the accumulation. Patterns across interviews that no one-off study can surface. Contradictions worth paying attention to. A growing sense of who the users actually were, week by week. That compound knowledge is what the team had been missing.

03 — The gap

Six weeks without sessions

Summer arrived. The Wednesday sessions paused. What started as a short break stretched to over a month. In the background, an experiment was already in flight.

The onboarding AI reel — a sequence of AI-generated video screens designed to give users a sensory first impression of the app — had been in preparation for weeks. It felt like a sure thing. We launched it into the A/B test without the user interviews we would normally have run first.

The results came back flat. And we had no way to understand why.

The lesson

We'd asked users what mattered to them, then built a static journey that ignored the answer. Without running sessions before launch, we had no way to see that coming.

04 — AI-moderated interviews

Scaling the diagnosis

When the Wednesday sessions resumed, I needed to move fast. The team was frustrated. We'd shipped something we believed in and had no explanation for why it hadn't worked.

I turned to AI-moderated interviews — a tool I'd been using throughout the tenure to extend our regular sessions. Genway earlier, Userology by this point. The capability was the same: send a structured usability test to new users, watch the recordings, reach saturation faster than weekly sessions alone could.

I used it alongside regular sessions — not instead of them. When we needed to cover other languages, or reach saturation quickly on a particular feature, AI moderation let us move at a different pace. This was the moment it mattered most.

The study

16 interviews, two rounds

I designed two sets of usability tests around a Figma prototype of the onboarding flow. Eight participants per round. The goal: watch real users move through the experience and find where it broke.

AI moderation let me run both rounds in a week — the kind of pace that weekly sessions over months couldn't match for a targeted diagnostic question.

What we found

The AI reel only resonated with users whose selected concern mapped to what they were then asked to configure. For everyone else, the experience felt confusing — we'd asked them what mattered, then ignored the answer. The experiment wasn't a bad idea. The assumption underneath it was.

05 — What the practice produced

A model the team could navigate by

The interviews weren't only for debugging. Across dozens of sessions, patterns emerged about how Sleep Cycle users actually moved through their relationship with the app — from curious newcomer to committed long-term user.

Those patterns became the Habit Loops model: a customer journey map built on interview evidence, cleaned up and launched internally as a strategic tool. It mapped how users moved from the trial phase through to the behaviours that defined long-term retention — and where they fell off.

From whiteboard to strategy tool

The cleaned-up version of the model — evolved from the sketch and validated against further interviews — was launched internally across the product team. It anchored decisions about onboarding, paywall messaging, and feature prioritisation for over a year.

Discovery informing craft

The next onboarding experiment — this time shaped by what the interviews had revealed about user expectations — achieved +6.7% uplift in subscription conversion in A/B testing. The discovery practice had done its job: not by generating a deck, but by giving the team a clearer picture of what actually mattered to users.

Outcome & reflection

The practice was the product

The most important thing the discovery practice produced wasn't any single insight or experiment result. It was a team that knew how to ask better questions before committing to solutions.

The summer gap was instructive in a way no internal presentation could have been. We felt the difference. Launching the onboarding reel without user input — and watching it fail without being able to explain why — made the value of the practice concrete in a way that metrics alone don't.

AI-moderated interviews gave us a new capability: the ability to ask focused, urgent questions at pace when the weekly cadence wasn't enough. Used alongside regular sessions, not instead of them, that combination changed what we could learn and how fast we could learn it.
